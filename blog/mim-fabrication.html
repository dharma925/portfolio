Fabricating TiO₂ MIM memory cells

Two metal–insulator–metal stacks, one insulator, and a measurable difference in how well each electrode metal made the switching behave.

For the VLSI Fabrication Practicum at IIT Mandi, my lab partner and I fabricated and characterized metal–insulator–metal (MIM) memristive cells using TiO₂ as the switching oxide, with two different top electrode metals: copper and aluminum. The goal was to build resistive-switching devices from scratch — cleanroom process to measured I–V curve — and see how the choice of electrode changed the result.

Structure

Both devices shared the same stack geometry: a platinum bottom electrode, a TiO₂ switching layer roughly 97 nm thick, and a top electrode of either copper or aluminum. So the comparison was clean — same oxide, same thickness, same process flow, only the top metal changed.

Process

The fabrication sequence:

  • RCA cleaning of the substrates to remove organic and ionic contamination before any deposition.
  • DC and RF sputtering to deposit the platinum bottom electrode and the TiO₂ oxide layer.
  • Annealing at 600°C to set the oxide's crystalline structure and stoichiometry.
  • Thermal deposition of the top electrode — copper on one device, aluminum on the other.

What the I–V curves showed

Both devices switched — applying a bias forced a transition from a high-resistance to a low-resistance state (SET), with the Cu/TiO₂/Pt device switching at a VSET of 4.1 V. But the two devices didn't switch equally well:

  • The Cu/TiO₂/Pt device showed an Ion/Ioff ratio up to 10⁴ — a clean, well-separated on/off window.
  • The Al/TiO₂/Pt device only reached an Ion/Ioff ratio of about 10² — two orders of magnitude worse.

The likely cause: aluminum forms a thin native Al₂O₃ layer at the interface with the TiO₂ almost as soon as it's deposited, before the stack is ever measured. That extra oxide interferes with the conductive filament formation that TiO₂ resistive switching depends on, which flattens the on/off contrast. Copper doesn't form a comparably stable native oxide under the same conditions, so the Cu device's switching stayed closer to the oxide's intrinsic behavior.

Takeaway

The electrode isn't a passive contact — its surface chemistry is part of the device. Two cells built from an identical oxide, on an identical bottom electrode, with an identical anneal, gave a 100x difference in switching ratio purely from which metal touched the oxide on top. We co-authored a lab report on the full characterization.
